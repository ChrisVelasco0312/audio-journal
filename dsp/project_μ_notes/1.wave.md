# 1. What is sound?

The rapid change in pressure (air) caused by mechanical vibrations (fluids and
solids). Clapping hands, the air between hands is forced out.
The air gets compressed, and to lower its energy, air moves outward causing the air farther from the source. 

Computers use conversions to process sound.

# Analog Signals.

First step: Convert pressure into ANALOG electrical signal using a transducer
(Microphone).

Analog Signal can be represented using math functions:

$$
  x(t)=\text{amplitude of signal at time t},
$$

Where time $t \in \mathbb{R}$ is a real number.

Reverse conversion is done using a speaker, which is driven by the electrical
signal $x(t)$ to produce mechanical vibrations.

# Digital Signals

Audio production and processing are done on a digital device.
Binary data has become the de facto way of storing and transmitting audio.
DSP is easier to develop, less dependent on temperature, component precision,
electromagnetic interference.

The conversion from analog signal to Digital (discrete-time) signal is done
using analog-to-digital converter ADC.

The reverse -> from binary encoding to continuous voltage, is done by
digital-to-analog DAC.

ADC -> recording audio.
DAC -> Playback.

## ADC implementation

1. Have an analog signal (continuous function) in mind
2. Sample it.
3. Hope it can be reconstructed with minimal distortion.

On a computer we mathematically construct an analog signal and sample it in code.

A correspondence between the input analog audio signal and its binary encoding
with two steps -> SAMPLING and QUANTIZATION

# Sampling

Can be viewed abstractly as:

$$
x[n] = x(nT)
$$

We obtain a new [discrete-time](discrete) signal $x[n]$ indexed by an index $n \in \mathbb{Z}$
from the original analog signal $x(t)$ indexed by time $t \in \mathbb{R}$ by
recording its value every $T\text{ seconds}$.

- T = SAMPLING PERIOD
- Unit is _seconds_
Is of fixed quantity.

**SAMPLING RATE** or **SAMPLE RATE** = $f_s$ in source code (`sr`)
Known also as **SAMPLING FREQUENCY**

Is the inverse of the sampling period
$$
f_s=\frac{1}{T}
$$

- It is the number of samples taken by the ADC every second.
- The unit of $f_s$ is samples/sec or Hertz (Hz).

It is possible to prove that sampling process is _theoretically lossless_ if
**FREQUENCY CONTENT** of the analog signal is less than $\frac{f_s}{2}$

This quantity is known as **NYQUIST FREQUENCY** and the result as
**Nyquist-Shannon** sampling theorem.

**Example:** The sampling rate of CD is `44000hz`. 
It can only capture analog audio signals with frequency content below the
Nyquist frequency: 
$$44100Hz / 2 = 22050Hz$$

Hearing human range is around 20-20000Hz. 

## Aliasing

Analog signals: $x(t)$ and $y(t)$ look different in the graph:
![[aliasing_signals_1.png]]
When we sample them using the same sampling period $T$, the two
sampled signals are exactly the same.

The two signals are **ALIAS** of each other under the sampling rate $f_s=\frac{1}{T}$

**ALIASING** occur because frequency of $x(t)$ is the Nyquist frequency $f_s/2$
We need to increase sampling rate to capture $x(t)$ unambiguously. 

$y(t)$ is not the only alias signal of  $x(t)$
![[aliasing_signals_2.png]]
We can produce the same samples from another signal $z(t)$ with an even higher frequency.
The frequency of $z(t)$ is above the Nyquist frequency.

It is neccesary to remove high frequency content of the analog signal
before sampling applying **low-pass filter**.

# Encoding and Quantization

To store sample value $x[n]$ as binary data
It is necessary to reduce its resolution and encode.
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wn71QBApCRg&t
An encoding used for audio is **Linear Pulse-Code Modulation (LPCM)**.
Encoded data is the amplitude of the analog signal sampled in uniform steps.

For 3-bit LPCM, the sample value $x[n]$ is quantized to $2^3 = 8$ discrete levels
![[quantized_1.png]]

For LPCM with bit depth <= 8 bits, the sample values are [[Unsigned]]
In C, the sampled digital signal can be represented as an array
`sample_t samples[] = {4, 6, 7, 7, 5, 3, 1, 1, 2, 4};`

`sample_t` is the data type of samples, a 3-bit unsigned integer.

"3-bit" here is called the **BIT DEPTH** of the encoding.
In CD audio, 16-bit LPCM is used, which provides $2^{16} = 65536$ different levels.
Studio record-id uses 24 bits $2^{24} = 16777216$ different levels .

Unlike sampling, quantization is lossy:
![[quantized_2.png]]
For signals with smaller amplitude, distortion will be more severe
![[quantized_3.png]]

The maximum deviation from the sample value to the quantized is 0.5 in both cases.
Deviation is also called **ROUND-OFF ERROR**

**ex:** 3.5 will be quantized to 4, and 3.49 will be quantized to 3
For the signal with lower amplitud, noise occupies a larger portion of the peak-to-peak amplitude of the signal, making the quantized signal less precise representation of the original.

The quantitative description of this using **signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) or signal-to-quantization-noise-ratio SQNR**, defined to be the power ratio or amplitude squared ratio between signal and noise

$$
SNR=\frac{A^2 signal}{A^2 noise}
$$
Where A = peak-to-peak amplitudes.

This ratio is represented in **decibel scale**
$$
SNRdB = 10log\frac{A^2signal}{A^2noise} = 20log\frac{Asignal}{Anoise}
$$
The peak-to-peak amplitude of the noise is $Anoise = 1$ 
The amplitude of the noise signal can be negative and the range is $[-0.5, 0.5]$ 
So peak-to-peak amplitud is not 0.5 but twice of that.

**First signal**. peak-to-peak amplitude is $Asignal=6$, where range is $[1, 7]$
SNR is:
$$
SNRdB = 20log\frac{6}{1} = 15.56dB
$$
**Second signal** $Asignal=2$ where range is [3, 5], SNR Is:
$$
SNRdB=20log\frac{2}{1} = 6.02dB
$$
The higher the better. So in second signal the adudio signal isn't very 
different from the quantization noise, and the audio sound very noisy.

The impact of quantization noises depends on the input signal level.
If input signal is weak, the signal might be masked by quantization noise.

The **bit-depth** also affect quantization noise
